      The method

      How a delivery becomes a player score

      Pressure is labelled before the ball is bowled, so the outcome can never decide whether a delivery counted.

      1. 1

        Estimate the stakes before the ball

        Innings-specific models estimate each side's expected result points (win 1, tie 0.5, loss 0) from the score, wickets, balls used, venue, team strength and target. Each scored year uses models fitted only to earlier years.

      2. 2

        Flag the high-pressure deliveries

        A ball is high pressure when the spread of result prospects across its plausible outcomes reaches the historical 75th percentile for that innings. The same pre-ball label applies to batter and bowler.

      3. 3

        Measure contribution against the situation

        Runs and wickets are valued against a scoreless, wicketless ball, then compared with what was historically expected in the same context: innings phase, opponent history and free-hit status.

      4. 4

        Estimate, compare and rank

        Each player's pressure performance P is estimated together with their ordinary baseline, pulled toward zero when pressure evidence is thin, and ranked. A conservative rank uses the lower 10th percentile across 1,000 resamples.

      Method details
      Estimate the stakes
      Separate models for each innings estimate expected regulation result points (win = 1, tie = 0.5, loss = 0). Inputs include score, wickets, balls used, venue, team strength and the chase target. Each scored year uses models fitted to earlier years.
      Label the delivery
      Estimate the spread (standard deviation) of result prospects across plausible next-ball outcomes. A ball qualifies when that spread reaches the historical 75th percentile for its innings. The same pre-ball label applies to both roles.
      Attribute the outcome
      Value attributed runs and wickets against a scoreless, wicketless event consuming the same legal-ball resource. Batters receive batter runs and dismissal costs. Bowling credit includes charged runs, credited wickets and the cost of granting a free hit. Run-outs earn no bowler wicket credit.
      Compare like situations
      Subtract a historical reference contribution conditional on context, including innings phase, opponent history and free-hit status. Positive residual credit means the player beat that reference on the delivery.
      Estimate player performance
      Estimate an ordinary baseline and the pressure difference jointly. Pull the difference toward zero when pressure evidence is sparse, then shrink both estimates by the same total-exposure factor. Pressure score P = ordinary baseline + uplift.
      Rank and qualify
      Order by estimated P, in adjusted result-point credit per 100 exposure balls. A separate conservative rank uses the tenth percentile of P over 1,000 competition-and-match resamples within years.
      Data rules
      Completed regulation ties are included. Revised, incomplete and anomalous games are excluded. Pressure measures competitive stakes; psychological stress is not observed.

        04
        Before the ball

        What pressure looks like before the ball

        Each bar is the spread of the batting side's result prospects across plausible next-ball outcomes. The navy marker is the historical cutoff for that innings, and all three bars share one scale. Read the situation and its label, then reveal the delivery.

        Late overs alone do not create pressure. When a chase is nearly settled, even a boundary barely moves the likely result. In the first innings, wickets and scoring chances can shift the projected result well before a target exists.
